# Ulu Lāhui Foundation

## Why We Exist

Ulu Lāhui Foundation exists to ensure Native Hawaiian communities have the knowledge, tools, infrastructure, and authority to determine and create the technologies that lead to thriving education, work, ʻāina, community life, and a sovereign people.

Hawaiʻi has always been a place of invention. Hawaiian communities developed sophisticated systems for navigation, agriculture, aquaculture, resource management, language, and collective stewardship in response to the opportunities and constraints of these islands. We believe that tradition of innovation should shape the technologies of the future.

## Mission

We build Native Hawaiian capacity to create, use, and govern technology through education, workforce development, applied research, and community-led innovation rooted in the knowledge, needs, and possibilities of Hawaiʻi.

## Vision

A Hawaiʻi where Native Hawaiian communities determine and create the technologies, systems, and opportunities that affect our people, ʻāina, and future generations, and where technologies born from Hawaiian knowledge and experience contribute solutions to challenges far beyond our islands.

# Strategic Pillars

## 1. Education & Learning Technology

### ʻAʻohe pau ka ʻike i ka hālau hoʻokahi.

*All knowledge is not taught in the same school. One can learn from many sources.*

**ʻŌlelo Noʻeau #203.** Mary Kawena Pukui, *ʻŌlelo Noʻeau: Hawaiian Proverbs & Poetical Sayings*, Bishop Museum Press, 1983.

**Build the knowledge, tools, and teaching capacity for Native Hawaiian learners and educators to become creators of technology, not simply users of it.**

Knowledge does not come from a single classroom, institution, technology, or tradition. Hawaiian communities already hold deep knowledge in language, culture, place, family, and our own engineering and practice. We bring those sources into conversation with computer science, AI, engineering, and emerging technology.

We develop culturally grounded computer science, AI, STEM, and learning technologies rooted in Hawaiian language, place, knowledge, and ways of learning. We prepare educators and students to understand emerging technologies critically, adapt them to local needs, and build new tools that reflect perspectives and priorities rarely represented in mainstream technology.

Hawaiʻi's schools and communities should not have to force themselves to fit into technologies designed elsewhere. They should help determine what educational technology looks like.

**Current work:** PLACES Education, Kumu Connect, AI for Hawaiʻi, bilingual CS/STEM curricula, teacher professional development, and Mālama ʻĀina through Micro:bits.

## 2. Workforce & Economic Opportunity

### Ma ka hana ka ʻike.

*In working, one learns.*

**ʻŌlelo Noʻeau #2088.** Mary Kawena Pukui, *ʻŌlelo Noʻeau: Hawaiian Proverbs & Poetical Sayings*, Bishop Museum Press, 1983. 

**Create pathways for Native Hawaiian people to build, operate, and lead the technologies and industries shaping Hawaiʻi's future.**

Technical capacity grows through doing. Our workforce programs emphasize hands-on learning, real projects, mentorship, work experience, and opportunities to build things that matter to communities.

We provide training, work-based learning, internships, and pathways into AI, computing, data, digital infrastructure, AgTech, and related fields. We connect emerging technology investment with local skills, careers, entrepreneurship, and ownership.

Our goal is not only to prepare Hawaiian people for jobs created elsewhere. It is to develop the technical capacity and leadership needed for new companies, systems, and industries to be built in Hawaiʻi by people who understand this place.

**Current work:** DHHL Data & AI workforce programs, digital and AI literacy, data center and infrastructure training, ʻĀina Intelligence Lab internships, Tinker with Tech programs, and future postsecondary and career pathways.

## 3. ʻĀina & Resilience

### He aliʻi ka ʻāina; he kauwā ke kanaka.

*The land is chief; people are its servants.*

**ʻŌlelo Noʻeau #531.** Mary Kawena Pukui, *ʻŌlelo Noʻeau: Hawaiian Proverbs & Poetical Sayings*, Bishop Museum Press, 1983. 

**Develop technology with the people whose knowledge and work sustain Hawaiʻi.**

Technology should strengthen our ability to care for ʻāina, not replace the relationships and knowledge that sustain it. It should support our practice in a changing world and climate. Farmers, fishers, cultural practitioners, and land stewards should not be users of technologies developed elsewhere. They are resilience stewards whose generations of experience can shape what new technologies should be.

Hawaiʻi's communities hold deep knowledge about managing complex ecological systems under conditions of limited land, water, energy, and resources. That knowledge can generate new approaches to sensing, AI, agriculture, food systems, environmental management, and resilience.

We combine place-based expertise with engineering and research capacity to build technologies suited to Hawaiʻi's realities. In doing so, Hawaiʻi can also produce solutions relevant to other communities confronting resource constraints, climate change, food insecurity, and environmental uncertainty.

**Current work:** Kilo Tech, loko iʻa and loʻi sensing, environmental monitoring, and place-based sensing systems.

## 4. Research & Technology

### Ua lehulehu a manomano ka ʻikena a ka Hawaiʻi.

*Great and numerous is the knowledge of the Hawaiians.*

**ʻŌlelo Noʻeau #2814.** Mary Kawena Pukui, *ʻŌlelo Noʻeau: Hawaiian Proverbs & Poetical Sayings*, Bishop Museum Press, 1983.

**Invent technologies that can emerge only from the knowledge, culture, relationships, and conditions of Hawaiʻi.**

Ancestors of modern Hawaiians developed a highly organized, self-sufficient, advanced agrarian society that, at its peak, provided food, shelter, entertainment, community, and culture for over 700,000 people. Ancestral and oral knowledge, called ʻike kūpuna, was cultivated, passed on, and built up over a millennium.

Hawaiian knowledge more than cultural context to add to research developed elsewhere. It is a source of questions, methods, designs, and inventions, and Hawaiʻi is more than a remote place where technologies developed elsewhere are tested or adapted.

Native Hawaiian knowledge systems, language, cultural practices, and histories of innovation create ways of seeing technical problems that are often absent from mainstream research. Hawaiʻi's island geography, ecological diversity, resource constraints, multilingual communities, and deep traditions of stewardship create challenges and opportunities that demand different approaches to AI, computing, sensing, infrastructure, and data.

We bring Hawaiian cultural knowledge together with advanced research and engineering to ask different questions and build different systems: AI grounded in Hawaiian language and epistemology; technologies designed around Indigenous data sovereignty; computing infrastructure built for local control; sensing systems informed by traditional environmental knowledge; and educational technologies shaped by Hawaiian ways of teaching and learning.

We conduct rigorous applied research, evaluate what we build, publish what can appropriately be shared, and translate research into technologies and programs that communities can own, govern, and extend.

**Current work:** Kumu Connect R&D, Hawaiian-language and low-resource AI, culturally grounded AI evaluation, Indigenous data sovereignty, sovereign and local AI infrastructure, edge computing, environmental sensing, language preservation technologies, and research collaborations with universities and community organizations.

# How We Work

The four pillars address different needs, but they share a common approach.

**Hawaiian leadership.** Native Hawaiian communities help determine the questions, priorities, technologies, and measures of success.

**Place creates knowledge.** Our people, culture, ecosystems, history, and constraints shape what we build and make forms of innovation possible that would not emerge elsewhere.

**Knowledge has many sources.** Academic and technical knowledge sit alongside ʻike Hawaiʻi, ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi, lived experience, community expertise, and knowledge developed through practice.

**Learning through doing.** We create opportunities for learners, educators, workers, and communities to gain capability by building, experimenting, maintaining, and solving real problems.

**Community co-design.** We build with educators, practitioners, learners, and communities from the beginning, recognizing them as experts and creators rather than recipients of technology.

**Culture and language throughout.** ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi, ʻike Hawaiʻi, place, and cultural context shape how problems are understood and how technologies are designed.

**Kuleana to ʻāina.** Technology should strengthen relationships among people, place, and future generations and support the people whose work sustains Hawaiʻi.

**Community control of data and technology.** We design for responsible data governance, local ownership, maintainability, technological sovereignty, and Indigenous data sovereignty.

**Bring resources home and build capacity here.** We connect Hawaiʻi communities with research expertise, technology, capital, and partnerships while ensuring that knowledge, infrastructure, and technical capacity remain in Hawaiʻi.

**Evidence and accountability.** We evaluate programs and technologies, publish findings where appropriate, measure outcomes, and change our approach when the evidence says we should.

**Build lasting capacity.** A successful project leaves people, skills, tools, infrastructure, knowledge, and relationships in place after the grant ends.

## Theory of Change

The four ʻōlelo noʻeau also describe how Ulu Lāhui approaches its work:

**ʻAʻohe pau ka ʻike i ka hālau hoʻokahi.**  
We recognize many sources of knowledge.

**Ma ka hana ka ʻike.**  
We develop knowledge and capability through practice.

**He aliʻi ka ʻāina; he kauwā ke kanaka.**  
We exercise that capability with responsibility to ʻāina.

**Ua lehulehu a manomano ka ʻikena a ka Hawaiʻi.**  
We recognize Hawaiian knowledge as a foundation for new research, technologies, and possibilities.

Together, they describe an approach to technology in which Hawaiian people are not just users, workers, research participants, or beneficiaries. **We are teachers, builders, stewards, researchers, inventors, and owners of our technological future.**


## Lencioni's six questions


| Question | ULF answer |
|---|---|
| **Why do we exist?** | To give Native Hawaiian communities greater capacity and authority to shape the technologies affecting their lives, ʻāina, and future. |
| **How do we behave?** | Hawaiian-led; community-accountable; collaborative; culturally grounded; rigorous; transparent; respectful of community knowledge and data. |
| **What do we do?** | Education and EdTech; workforce pathways; ʻāina/resilience technology; applied research and technology development. |
| **How will we succeed?** | Build with communities; combine research with implementation; bring external technical resources into Hawaiʻi; develop local people and infrastructure; create reusable programs rather than one-off projects; measure outcomes. |
| **What is most important right now?** | **Turn successful projects into durable programs.** Establish clear ownership, repeatable delivery models, measurable outcomes, multi-year funding, and the operational capacity to grow without becoming a collection of unrelated grants. |
| **Who must do what?** | Board guards mission and governance; executive leadership sets strategy, partnerships, and capital; program leads own delivery and outcomes; community/cultural leads own co-design and cultural accountability; technical/research teams build and evaluate; operations owns compliance, data, finance, and reporting. |
