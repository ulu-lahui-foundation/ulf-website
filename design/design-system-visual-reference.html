Part 1 — Concept

Fill, shadow, stitch

The same ulu (breadfruit) leaf appears three times, as three translations of itself: fill, a solid appliquéd shape — knowledge as it exists, whole and certain. Shadow, a dim displaced silhouette — the moment of transmission, observed and translated. Stitch, a running-thread outline — what's re-made by a different hand. One idea about how knowledge moves, expressed as a system that shows up in a button's states as readily as in the motif itself.

Part 2 — The site as a quilt

Piecing and appliqué are two different jobs

Appliqué lays one motif on top of a field, stitched around its own edge — that's the leaf, fill/shadow/stitch, and it's flat: a button is a cut shape held down by thread, not a padded block (Part 7). Piecing joins separate fabric patches at a seam pressed flat into a fine line — that's the layout. A group of related content is unevenly sized blocks pushed edge to edge; the gap between them is a seam, not a border on any one block; only the outer edge of the whole pieced group gets a radius, the way a quilt is bound at its edge but no single patch is.

One more layer: once the patches are pieced, a real quilt gets quilted straight through everything — top, batting, backing — so each patch ends up puffed even though the seam between patches stays flat and pressed. Containers here work the same way (Part 6): the seam is structural and flat, the patch surface on either side of it is not.

Part 3 — Color

Six hues, three depths each — no white, no black

Every color comes from the same dye bath at a different strength: a light wash, the full-strength dye, and a deep over-dyed version. That's a light field, a vivid accent, and a dark field, for each of six hues. Backgrounds are always one of these eighteen — never a neutral white or gray. Text and stitching use Kukui ink, a dark warm brown with red in it, never a flat black.

ʻAlaea is now the confirmed primary, #D96913 — pulled directly from the logo (which actually renders #D86911, near-identical, likely a compression rounding difference). Ulu is now #A2C601, a much brighter yellow-green than the earlier forest tone — bright enough that it needs Kukui ink for its text, not white. One more data point from the logo: its gold sits at #F0C05D, a touch lighter and more yellow than the ʻŌlena below — worth considering if ʻŌlena should shift to match it exactly, but not changed here without confirmation.

Kukui ink — #2B1B12 — default text, seams, and stitch lines. Warm, dark, never neutral.

Usage ratio: light and vivid carry the page; dark is for text, seams, and thin lines only. A dark field (the third column above) is the rare exception, not a default background choice — reach for it sparingly, if at all, rather than defaulting to it for hero sections, footers, or any large surface. Most of a page should read as pastel fields and confident vivid accents, with Kukui ink doing the quiet work of text and structure underneath.

Headers on a light field use that hue's vivid tone as text color, not a muted dark tint — a headline should read as confidently colored, not softened into near-gray safety. Two exceptions, purely for contrast: ʻŌlena and Ulu are both light, bright hues, so their vivid tone sits too close in value to their own light wash to read clearly as text (checked, not guessed: under 2:1 contrast). For those two only, use the dark tint for headers; every other hue uses vivid text directly on its light field. Body copy can stay on the dark tint throughout for easier reading at length — this rule is about headers and labels, not paragraphs. Vivid containers next to other vivid containers are also fine and encouraged (Part 8) — there's no rule requiring a light field to buffer every vivid one.

Part 6 — Surfaces are not flat

Trapunto, not a drop shadow

Quilts get dimension from batting and thread, not from light and blur — a trapunto-stitched motif is visibly puffed, raised above the surrounding field, because there's padding stuffed behind it. That's a mechanical, physical kind of dimension, and it's a different thing from a generic UI drop shadow. So instead of a soft outer glow, every filled surface here gets a tight inset bevel: a thin light catch on one edge, a thin dark recess on the other, like fabric pressed down around something puffed underneath. Paired with a faint directional gradient (a dye bath is never perfectly even), it reads as real material without reaching for the purple-glow, glass-card AI default.

This bevel belongs to containers — cards, callouts, gallery cells, pieced blocks — never to controls. Buttons are appliqué (Part 7): flat, held down by stitching, no padding underneath. A pieced block is different from a button — after piecing, a real quilt gets quilted straight through all the joined patches, so each patch is puffed even though the seam between patches stays a flat, pressed line. That's the distinction that matters here: the seam is structural and flat; the patch surface on either side of it is not.

Part 7 — Buttons

Primary, secondary, tertiary — each with four states

Every button is appliqué (Part 2) — flat, held down by a stitched border, never padded or bevelled the way a container is (Part 6). The dashed border isn't decoration; it's the same running stitch that holds any appliquéd shape to its field. Hierarchy is fill: primary is solid, secondary is outlined, tertiary is just colored text.

Primary

Fill is any vivid — whichever fits the surrounding block, same as everywhere else in this system. Border is that vivid's own deep tone, one shade darker, dashed. Hover and active are identical: an inset shadow, like the button is being pressed into the fabric it's stitched to.

Part 8 — Stitched and patches

Two physical states, two rule sets

Before the worked examples, the rules themselves. Content on this site exists in one of two physical states, and each behaves differently — this section is the spec; Part 9 is it applied.

Stitched is pieced together and then quilted straight through, edge to edge, into one complete finished quilt spanning the page. Patches are cut pieces that haven't been sewn to anything yet — loose, individual, sitting on their own. Part 9 shows five layouts, each built both ways.

Stitched — rules

  • The seam is a plain solid border, one shade darker than the container's own shadow. No dashes — dashes only ever come from the shadow color underneath each block anyway, so a solid line one step darker reads as a clean pressed edge without extra ornament. Each container carries its own 4px rounded corner too — the radius isn't reserved for the outer edge of a group.
  • Gutters get the exact same treatment as the blocks they frame — same puffy bevel, same sharp un-rounded corners. A gutter is a patch of the same finished quilt, not a separate frame bolted onto it, so it can't have different edge treatment than its neighbors.
  • Mix vivids freely — that's the fun of a finished quilt. ʻŌlena with Lehua, Moana with Ulu, and most other combinations read well side by side. Two specific pairs don't: ʻAlaea next to Moana, and ʻAlaea next to ʻŌlena both go muddy where they touch. Avoid those two; everything else is fair game, including vivid-on-vivid with no light buffer between (Part 3).

Patches — rules

  • A real outer drop shadow, not the inset trapunto bevel. A patch hasn't been quilted yet — there's no batting behind it to puff it up. What it does have is weight: it's a cut piece of fabric sitting loose on a table, casting an ordinary shadow below it.
  • A few patches sit at a slight angle — not perfectly aligned to the grid. Scattered pieces on a table are never all square to each other; a couple of degrees of rotation here and there keeps them looking placed, not printed.
  • Corners are fully sharp — zero radius, no rounding at all. This is sharper than the 4px used everywhere else in the system: raw cut fabric has a hard edge, full stop.

Part 10 — Section transitions

Moving between stitched and patches on the actual page

Part 9 shows each layout stitched and as patches in isolation, for comparison. On the real site, a page will move from one to the other — a patches gallery followed by a stitched callout, say. That handoff needs its own rule, or it just reads as the page ending arbitrarily.

The transition lives in the patches color, #FBEACA, since that's the "table" both states share. A solid line marks the actual seam, colored and weighted to match the patches shadow exactly: #492306 at 0.35 opacity, the same warm dark brown used under every patch, not a neutral black or gray:

  • Patches → stitched: no line at all. The stitched piece's own border already marks where it starts — an extra line on top of that reads as redundant.
  • Stitched → patches: an 8px line. A visible break, since a patch has no border of its own to mark the seam — the line has to do that work alone.

Part 12 — Layout, spacing, voice

Rhythm and words

Echo spacing. 4 · 8 · 16 · 24 · 40 · 64 · 96 px, used unevenly on purpose — the same gap everywhere reads as no rhythm, just repetition.

Voice. Active voice, first-person plural. No em dashes. Hawaiian terms in italics with an English gloss on first use. Community members as agents, not beneficiaries. No "not X but Y" constructions. Plain and warm, not clever.

Where "add dimension" stops and "AI slop" starts
